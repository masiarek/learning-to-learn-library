# Goals and schedules: what makes a goal one you keep, and what a week of study looks like

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who starts every semester with a resolution and ends it scrambling

**One line:** Specific, measurable goals just below your best set the pace; process goals get you there and are the ones people forget; a written schedule with an hour in reserve is what makes the goals survive contact with a week. The goal-setting research is among the best replicated in psychology; the daily protocols built on top of it are sensible templates, not findings.

## Goals

Zakrajsek's *The New Science of Learning*, chapter 3, opens on students who go to class, study, take tests and repeat, and observes that this is not a plan. What the research says a plan needs:

- **Specific, challenging goals beat easy goals and "do your best".** Locke and Latham's programme, hundreds of studies over forty years, is the evidence. A good goal sits just below the best you expect, about 90% of it: an exam goal of 90% rather than 100%, which one missed question would break, and rather than "study", which nothing can break or meet. *Grade: well established.*
- **SMART**: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, timely. "Do well in all my classes" fails the first two; "maintain a B− average on all assignments and exams" passes. Study Tip 3.1: set one SMART goal and one SMART *outcome*, what you will be able to do at the end, for every study session. An outcome such as "list the structures of the limbic system without notes" is a test you can take, which is what [metacognition](../../01_Knowing_What_You_Know/metacognition/README.md) asks for. *Grade: the acronym is a mnemonic; the parts it names are the well-established ones above.*
- **Process goals and end goals.** "Get an A in statistics" is an end goal; "study three hours a day, five days a week" is the process goal that gets you there, and the one people forget (Latham and Brown, 2006). *Grade: good evidence.*
- **Goal creep.** A vague goal drifts: you study hard, do well on the first exams, skip a session, still do well, skip more, and finish the semester scrambling. A measurable goal shows the drift while it is small.
- **Break large goals into smaller ones.** Short subgoals give feedback often, which is the start-small remedy of [learned helplessness](../../03_Believing_You_Can/learned_helplessness/README.md) applied before the helplessness sets in.
- **Stretch goals**: a chance of success around 10%, still possible. Study Tip 3.2: set one per exam, and only one. A record made mostly of missed goals teaches you to stop trying, and the book's point is that a missed stretch goal still accomplishes a lot. *Do the math* on its own example: a million steps in a school year of about 32 weeks is roughly 4,500 steps a day.

## The schedule

- **Write it down**, with every deadline from every syllabus, study blocks treated like meetings, time for yourself, and **an hour in reserve every day**. [The buffer hour](../the_buffer_hour/README.md) is the arithmetic of why that hour is not pessimism: task times are skewed, so a day planned to capacity fits about one time in thirteen.
- **Learn to say no**, which a full calendar makes easy: thank them, and say the calendar has no room.
- **Study at consistent times**, so the habit carries the plan on days when motivation does not. *Grade: good evidence for habit formation; the timing itself is personal.*
- **Chronotype.** People differ in when they are most alert, and demanding work goes better in your own peak. *Grade: good evidence that chronotypes exist and matter for performance; weak for any rule finer than "know yours".*
- **Blocks and breaks.** A block of about an hour with a real break, away from the screen, is a sensible unit. The claim that attention runs in 90-minute ultradian cycles, so blocks should be 90 minutes, is weak ([brain chemistry](../../05_Body_and_Brain/brain_chemistry/README.md#what-does-not-follow)); the Pomodoro rule of 25 minutes has no experimental basis of its own either, but breaks do. *Grade: good evidence for breaks; weak for any particular length.*

## To-do lists

Zakrajsek's rules, each of which this library can say something about:

1. **Write a task down as soon as you think of it**, and stop holding it in working memory, which has about four places ([cognitive load](../../02_Making_It_Stay/cognitive_load/README.md)).
2. **Give it a date; then one extension; then ask whether it needs doing at all.**
3. **A two-minute task, do now.**
4. **Review the list morning and evening.**
5. **Estimate each item's time.** "As you estimate, your estimates will improve", he writes, adding "I still struggle with this." They improve only if you keep score: [the buffer hour](../the_buffer_hour/README.md) turns the record into a ratio you multiply new estimates by.
6. **Anything over 30 minutes goes straight into the calendar**, so the list and the schedule stay one system.
7. **A task is done when its product is filed**, not when you press send, as dinner is done when the dishes are put away.

## A week, as a template

The daily protocols that circulate (morning: light, water, movement, new material; afternoon: retrieval and interleaved practice, then exercise; evening: spaced review, plan tomorrow, wind down; Sunday: plan the week; Friday: test yourself on the week) are reasonable ways to arrange the things this library recommends. They are templates, not findings: no study has compared one such day against another. What is established is the ingredients, and a week that contains them in any arrangement you will keep is a good week:

| Ingredient | How often | Why |
|---|---|---|
| retrieval of yesterday's material | every day, 10 minutes, before new material | [spaced retrieval](../../02_Making_It_Stay/spaced_retrieval/README.md): the first review while the trace is fresh |
| new material, with questions written before reading | in your peak hours | [count the vowels](../../01_Knowing_What_You_Know/count_the_vowels/README.md), pretesting |
| mixed practice | every session that has problems | [interleaving](../../02_Making_It_Stay/interleaving/README.md) |
| a walk or other exercise | most days | [exercise](../../05_Body_and_Brain/exercise/README.md): attention and mood for the next hour, sleep tonight |
| a self-test on the week | once a week | [metacognition](../../01_Knowing_What_You_Know/metacognition/README.md): a test, not a feeling, ends the week |
| planning next week, with the buffer hour | once a week | this page |
| sleep | every night | [sleep](../../05_Body_and_Brain/sleep/README.md): the consolidation happens then |

McGuire's version is the **study cycle**, on [spaced retrieval](../../02_Making_It_Stay/spaced_retrieval/README.md#the-study-cycle-spacing-built-into-a-week): preview, class, review the same day, three or four intense study sessions of an hour with a break, and a self-test.

## Talking to faculty

Zakrajsek, a first-generation student himself, ends the chapter on office hours: listen actively; before you leave, summarise what you heard in ten or fifteen seconds; ask before taking notes on a phone; and ask for clarification rather than say you understand when you do not. The last is the first chapter's rule again: "I understand" is a prediction, and the assignment is the test.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Konkretny, mierzalny cel tuż poniżej twojego maksimum działa lepiej niż „postaram się” (Locke i Latham, setki badań). Cele procesowe („trzy godziny dziennie, pięć dni w tygodniu”) prowadzą do celów końcowych („piątka ze statystyki”) i to o nich ludzie zapominają. Cel ambitny z szansą około 10% warto mieć jeden, nie więcej. Plan tygodnia ma być zapisany, z blokami nauki traktowanymi jak spotkania i godziną rezerwy dziennie. Lista zadań: zapisz od razu, daj termin, potem jedno przedłużenie, potem pytanie, czy w ogóle trzeba; szacuj czas i sprawdzaj szacunki. Codzienne „protokoły” krążące w internecie to rozsądne szablony, nie wyniki badań; ważne są składniki, nie kolejność.
