# Food and supplements: does anything you eat make you learn faster?

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has been offered a "nootropic stack"

**One line:** Being fed, hydrated and not short of a vitamin matters; a caffeinated drink improves alertness; and beyond that the evidence for any food or supplement improving a healthy adult's learning is thin, while the evidence against "smart drugs" for healthy people is fairly good. This page names no doses and recommends nothing to take: those are questions for a doctor.

## What is well established

- **Caffeine improves alertness and attention**, and reduces the effects of tiredness, for a few hours. It is the most studied stimulant there is. Its half-life of five to six hours is why an afternoon coffee reaches into the night ([sleep](../sleep/README.md)). *Grade: well established.*
- **Going without food, water or sleep makes you worse at everything cognitive.** Skipping breakfast lowers attention in children in many studies; even mild dehydration lowers attention and mood in adults. *Grade: well established for the deficits; the fix is to eat and drink normally.*
- **A deficiency of a B vitamin, iron or iodine impairs cognition, and correcting it helps.** The word that matters is *deficiency*: extra beyond normal levels does nothing measurable. *Grade: well established.*

## What has good evidence

- **A Mediterranean-style diet goes with slower cognitive decline in later life**, in observational studies and one large trial (PREDIMED). The evidence is about decades and older adults, not about this semester. *Grade: good evidence for long-term brain health; nothing specific to learning now.*
- **Caffeine after learning improved consolidation** in one well-known experiment (Borota and colleagues, 2014). It has not been widely replicated. Becker and Mills (2022, [doi:10.1002/hbm.25949 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1002/hbm.25949)) compared caffeine with methylphenidate and modafinil in healthy adults and found small effects on memory and on resting brain networks for all three. *Grade: good evidence for caffeine's alertness effect; weak for a memory effect beyond it.*
- **Heavy meals before intellectual work make you sluggish.** *Grade: good evidence, and common experience.*

## What is weaker

- **Omega-3 supplements** for cognition in healthy adults: large reviews find no benefit. Eating fish is part of the diet above. *Grade: weak to none.*
- **Caffeine with L-theanine.** A handful of small, short, often industry-funded studies find slightly better attention and fewer jitters than caffeine alone. *Grade: weak.*
- **Bacopa monnieri, Ginkgo biloba, curcumin, "green apple flavonoids".** Bacopa has small trials with mixed results over months of use. Ginkgo's largest trial (the GEM study, 2008, over 3,000 people) found no effect on memory or dementia. The Ginkgo-and-mushroom study in the guide's list (Hong and colleagues, 2022, [doi:10.1155/2022/9973678 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/9973678)) is in mice given a drug that induces amnesia, and the apple-juice study ([doi:10.47583/IJPSRR.2021.V67I01.002 ↗](https://doi.org/10.47583/IJPSRR.2021.V67I01.002)) is in mice too. Malik and Tlustoš's review of nootropics (2022, [doi:10.3390/nu14163367 ↗](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14163367)) lists many such compounds and, read carefully, finds the human evidence thin for nearly all. *Grade: weak, and none of it about healthy learners.*
- **Choline-rich foods "to make acetylcholine".** Choline is a normal nutrient and eggs are food; there is no evidence that eating more of it than usual improves attention. *Grade: speculative.*
- **Caloric restriction and fasting.** Improves memory and plasticity in rodents. In people, the studies are about weight and metabolism, not learning. *Grade: weak for learners.*
- **A high-fat, high-sugar diet impairs memory within days.** Well shown in rats; one small human study (Attuquayefio and colleagues, 2017) found a hippocampus-dependent memory task worse after four days of such a diet. *Grade: weak but plausible.*

## Prescription stimulants and "smart drugs"

Methylphenidate, amphetamines and modafinil are medicines for diagnosed conditions. In healthy people, reviews find small and inconsistent effects on memory and attention, mostly when tired, alongside real risks: dependence, cardiovascular strain, sleep loss that costs more memory than the drug gains (Kapur, 2020, [doi:10.1007/s40263-020-00758-w ↗](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40263-020-00758-w), on methylphenidate; Sharif and colleagues, 2021, [doi:10.3390/brainsci11030355 ↗](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci11030355), on university students; Schifano and colleagues, 2022, [doi:10.1007/s40265-022-01701-7 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40265-022-01701-7), on benefits and harms). Donepezil, a dementia drug, does nothing useful for healthy memory. The only advice this library gives about any of them is the one every review gives: not without a doctor, and not as a substitute for the techniques in chapters 01 to 04, which are free and work.

## What to do

1. **Eat normally, drink water, do not skip meals on study days.** The gains available here are from avoiding deficits, not from adding things.
2. **Use caffeine for alertness if you use it at all, early in the day**, and count the sleep cost.
3. **Spend the supplement money on sleep and time** instead. Nothing on this page beats an extra hour of either.
4. **Ask a doctor** before taking anything to improve concentration, and be suspicious of anyone selling a stack.
