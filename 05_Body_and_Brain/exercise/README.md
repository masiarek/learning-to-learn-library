# Exercise: what a walk does for attention, and what BDNF is

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has read that a run before studying "primes the brain"

**One line:** Regular aerobic exercise is one of the best-supported things anyone can do for the brain they will have in thirty years, and a bout of it improves attention and mood for the next hour or two. The claim that a run just before or after studying makes that session's learning stick is real but small and inconsistent, and the BDNF story that usually comes with it is a mechanism seen mostly in blood tests and rodents.

## What is well established

- **Regular aerobic exercise is good for the brain over years.** In older adults, a year of walking increased hippocampal volume and improved memory against a stretching control (Erickson and colleagues, 2011), and observational studies link lifelong activity to slower cognitive decline. *Grade: well established for long-term brain health; the direct evidence is strongest in older adults.*
- **A single bout of moderate exercise improves attention and executive function for an hour or two afterwards**, and mood with it. Meta-analyses of acute exercise agree on a small to moderate effect (Chang and colleagues, 2012). *Grade: well established.*
- **Exercise improves sleep**, which [sleep](../sleep/README.md) turns into memory. *Grade: well established.*
- **Public-health amounts**: about 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75 vigorous, plus some resistance training. These are guidelines for health in general; there is no separate, tested dose for learning. *Grade: well established as health guidance.*

## What has good evidence

- **A bout of exercise around a learning session helps memory for that material a little.** Roig and colleagues' meta-analysis (2013) found small effects on long-term memory, larger for exercise *after* learning than before, and the studies since disagree about timing and intensity. Li and colleagues (2020, [doi:10.1007/s00213-020-05554-4 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-020-05554-4)) review evidence that exercise reduces proactive interference, the old material getting in the way of new. Amico, Braun and Schaefer (2021, [doi:10.1007/s12144-021-02352-9 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-021-02352-9)) asked whether a bout of resistance exercise helps encoding and found, at most, a small effect. *Grade: good evidence for a small, inconsistent benefit.*
- **Exercise raises catecholamines and BDNF in the blood for a while afterwards.** This is measured reliably. What is not established is the next step, that this rise changes what you learn in the following hour. *Grade: well established for the blood levels; weak for the link to a study session.*

## What is weaker, or mostly from animals

- **"BDNF is fertiliser for the brain."** Brain-derived neurotrophic factor supports neurons and synapses, and running raises it in the rodent hippocampus along with the birth of new neurons there, which improves rodent learning (a 2020 correction to Berdugo-Vega and colleagues, [doi:10.1038/s41467-020-14935-4 ↗](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14935-4), belongs to that literature). Human adult neurogenesis is itself still debated, and no one has shown a student's BDNF predicting their exam. *Grade: good evidence in animals; speculative as advice.*
- **"Do cardio right before a complex learning session to prime encoding."** Given the mixed timing results above, the honest version is: exercise at whatever time you will keep doing it, and expect attention and mood to benefit more than memory for that session. *Grade: weak.*
- **Exercise and the body clock.** Timed exercise can shift circadian rhythms, mostly shown in animals and in shift-work studies. *Grade: weak for learners.*
- **Intense exercise late in the evening harms sleep.** A 2019 meta-analysis (Stutz and colleagues) found evening exercise does not harm sleep on average, except vigorous exercise within about an hour of bedtime. *Grade: good evidence that the worry is overstated.*

## What to do

1. **Move most days**, for the brain you will have later and the sleep you will have tonight. Any activity you will keep doing beats the theoretically optimal one you will not.
2. **Use a short walk as a break** between study blocks: attention and mood improve for the next hour, which is the one effect you can count on.
3. **Do not reorganise your studying around BDNF.** If exercising after a session is convenient, fine; the effect on that session is small either way.
4. **Combine it with something to learn** (a sport, a dance, a route) if you like; the "enriched environment" evidence is from animals, but it costs nothing.

## Po polsku, w skrócie

Regularny ruch to jedna z najlepiej potwierdzonych rzeczy, jakie można zrobić dla mózgu za trzydzieści lat, a pojedynczy spacer poprawia uwagę i nastrój na godzinę lub dwie. Twierdzenie, że bieg tuż przed nauką albo po niej utrwala tę konkretną sesję, jest prawdziwe, ale efekt jest mały i niespójny. Historia o BDNF („nawóz dla mózgu”) opiera się głównie na badaniach krwi i na gryzoniach; nikt nie pokazał, że poziom BDNF studenta przewiduje jego egzamin. Rada: ruszaj się codziennie, używaj krótkiego spaceru jako przerwy między blokami nauki i nie przebudowuj planu nauki wokół biochemii.
